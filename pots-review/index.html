Abstract

Background. Longitudinal phenotyping describes how a person's symptoms, findings, function, and clinical context change over time. Electronic health records (EHRs) can support this task, but their timing and content also reflect how care is delivered. Pediatric postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) makes these distinctions especially important: a diagnostic label, an upright heart-rate response, and sustained functional impairment describe different aspects of illness.

Approach. This targeted narrative review examines verified primary studies and selected clinical guidance available by 22 October 2026. It distinguishes ontology-based similarity, knowledge graphs, graph neural networks, temporal models, and multimodal embeddings. It is not a systematic review or a meta-analysis.

Findings. Prior work already includes longitudinal Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) analyses in pediatric epilepsy, ontology-informed EHR embeddings, cross-encounter graph learning, and integration of notes with structured records. POTS studies also include physiological machine learning and long-term outcome research. These findings rule out broad claims that longitudinal graph phenotyping or computational POTS assessment is wholly new. The reviewed evidence does not establish an externally validated, ontology-informed multimodal model of pediatric POTS trajectories that improves prediction of patient function beyond strong clinical and temporal baselines.

Implications. A useful next study should preserve symptom timing and evidence provenance, distinguish unknown from absent findings, compare graph models with simpler alternatives using identical inputs, and test transfer across sites and calendar periods. Its main outcome should concern subsequent function or symptom burden. Clusters should remain descriptive until replicated and independently characterized.

Central conclusion. The defensible opportunity is careful clinical validation of time-resolved POTS phenotypes. Combining an ontology, a graph, and a neural network is already established methodological territory.

Keywords: longitudinal phenotyping; Human Phenotype Ontology; knowledge graph; graph neural network; multimodal EHR; pediatric POTS.

1. Scope and how to read the evidence

This review asks which existing methods could represent changing patient phenotypes, what their evaluations actually establish, and what remains uncertain for pediatric POTS. Searches used combinations of longitudinal phenotyping, HPO embedding, temporal graph, knowledge graph, multimodal EHR, and POTS, followed by exact-title checks. Sources included PubMed, publisher pages, proceedings, author manuscripts, and institutional publication records. Bibliographic years refer to publication records, not search-engine crawl dates. Appendix A records the search scope and access limits.

Evidence is separated into three categories: reported findings from the cited studies; reviewer interpretation of their relevance and limitations; and the proposed protocol in Sections 7–9. Reported metrics describe each paper's own task. They do not form a shared benchmark, and no pooled effect or ranking is justified.

2. Concepts, step by step

1. A phenotype is an observed characteristic. Symptoms, examination findings, physiological measurements, and functional limitations can all contribute. A computable phenotype is a reproducible rule or algorithm for identifying a clinical condition or characteristic from data. A diagnosis code is one observation about recorded care; it is not automatically a verified diagnosis.

2. An ontology defines concepts and their relationships. The HPO standardizes abnormal phenotypic features and relationships between broader and more specific concepts. Phenopackets provide a structure for person-level observations, including onset, resolution, severity, and explicitly excluded features. Neither alone estimates a patient's future course.[1][2]

3. A knowledge graph stores typed connections. Nodes can represent patients, encounters, findings, treatments, and concepts. Edges specify relations such as “observed at,'' “mapped to,'' or “preceded.'' An ontology supplies definitions; a patient graph supplies individual observations. A co-occurrence graph merely records that items appear together. These are different sources of information and should remain distinguishable.

4. An embedding is a numeric representation. A concept or patient is represented by a vector, a list of numbers learned to preserve selected relationships or predict an outcome. Nearby vectors need not mean similar causes. Their meaning depends on the data and training objective. An average of all historical findings loses ordering unless timing is explicitly retained.

5. A graph neural network (GNN) learns from connected nodes. It repeatedly combines a node's information with information from selected neighbors. This is called message passing. Different edge types can receive different transformations. Graph embeddings based on random walks, ontology attention, and GNNs are related approaches, but they are not interchangeable names.

6. A longitudinal model represents repeated observations. It should account for elapsed time, age, and what was observable at each assessment. A transformer learns weighted relationships among inputs. A recurrent model updates a stored representation as observations arrive. Either can model time without an explicit graph. Multimodal models combine different kinds of data, such as notes, measurements, codes, and patient reports.

7. Three tasks require different evidence. Case identification asks whether a condition is present. Trajectory description asks how measured features change. Prognosis asks what occurs after a defined prediction time. Successful diagnosis-code prediction does not, by itself, validate a biological subtype or a treatment recommendation.

3. What prior work has established

Ontologies and pediatric longitudinal phenotyping

HPO2Vec+ enriched HPO node embeddings with disease–phenotype knowledge from DECIPHER, OMIM, and Orphanet. Its evaluation concerned phenotypic relevance, including precision, recall, and F1. This is evidence for concept representation, not direct evidence for tracking a child's changing state.[3]

Ganesan and colleagues provided a direct pediatric longitudinal precedent in 2020: 62,104 coded observations from 658 people with genetic epilepsies were mapped to HPO and grouped into three-month age intervals. The analysis identified time-dependent gene–phenotype associations across 3,251 patient-years. The method did not require a GNN. Restrictions to neurological codes and problems, inability to assess negation, and variable EHR coverage limited interpretation.[4] This work makes “adding time to ontology phenotyping'' an insufficient novelty claim.

Daniali and colleagues' 2023 frequency-based HPO embeddings used frequencies derived from 53 million notes from more than 1.5 million individuals to weight the ontology graph before Node2Vec+ embedding. Evaluation included expert judgments and patient similarity; the patient-pair analysis involved 53 people. The study reported 68% agreement with its expert-majority prioritization standard.[5] The large note corpus should therefore not be confused with a similarly large independent clinical validation cohort. Extraction artifacts and a largely static representation constrain transfer to prognosis.

MUGS, published in November 2024, combined ontology hierarchy with pediatric and general-system EHR co-occurrence information. It used 0.25 million Boston Children's Hospital and 2.5 million Mass General Brigham patients. The method separates group, shared-code, and site-specific effects, providing a concrete alternative to assuming that adult representations transfer unchanged. Evaluation included code relatedness and pediatric pulmonary hypertension phenotyping; 419 patients had expert labels.[6] These results are relevant to pediatric adaptation, but aggregated code profiles do not establish POTS trajectory states.

Ontology-informed prediction and learned graphs

GRAM (KDD 2017; preprint 2016) combines a medical code with its ontology ancestors using attention, then models visit sequences. Evaluation included sequential diagnosis prediction and heart-failure prediction, with particular motivation for infrequent codes.[7] It demonstrates that ontology information can regularize prediction. Its learned attention does not identify a causal mechanism.

Graph Convolutional Transformer (GCT; AAAI 2020; preprint 2019) uses data statistics to guide learning of EHR structure and evaluates graph reconstruction and clinical prediction, including readmission.[8] This differs from a model operating exclusively on curated ontology edges: the learned relations may encode documentation or treatment patterns.

MedPath (WWW 2021) retrieves personalized medical knowledge paths to augment risk models. Its author record reports evaluation on three datasets and improvements in F1 and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) across eight existing predictors.[9] Paths provide inspectable associations. Calling a path a disease-progression path does not prove that a patient actually passed through those states or that intervention on an intermediate node changes outcomes.

Together, these studies motivate testing explicit relationships. They do not justify assuming that more edges, a larger ontology, or a more complex network will help a smaller specialty cohort.

Temporal graphs, sequence models, and multimodal data

DeepJ extends GCT with temporal encoding, a mask restricting information flow, and graph pooling across encounters. The 2025 AMIA proceedings paper was posted in February 2026, following a June 2025 preprint. It evaluated ICU mortality in eICU (39,874 patients) and acute kidney injury at the University of Kansas Medical Center (25,955 patients). Reported AUROCs were 0.8933 and 0.7265, respectively.[10] This is direct evidence that cross-encounter graphs already exist, although inpatient prediction over short windows differs from outpatient symptom trajectories over years.

EHRFormer, published as an accepted article in August 2026, constructs temporal heterogeneous patient graphs and uses time-aware message passing for next-visit diagnosis prediction. Its reported evaluation uses MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV, and MarketScan CCAE, with precision@k and accuracy@k.[11] Those ranking metrics address recorded codes, not calibration, function, or biological subtypes. The retrieved version exposed an abstract-level methods and results account, so detailed split and leakage safeguards could not be audited here.

Strong nongraph comparators are essential. BEHRT (2020) models diagnoses with age, position, and visit-related information and evaluates future diagnoses in nearly 1.6 million people.[12] Med-BERT (2021; preprint 2020) pretrains on structured EHRs from 28,490,650 patients and evaluates heart failure among people with diabetes and pancreatic cancer prediction.[13] Their reported gains justify considering pretraining, but do not establish pediatric POTS validity. RETAIN (2016) offers an earlier visit- and variable-attention baseline.[14]

MedFuse (Hayat et al., 2022) combines clinical time series with chest radiographs through a recurrent fusion module. It evaluates mortality and phenotype classification using MIMIC-IV and MIMIC-CXR, including missing-image settings.[15] The transferable lesson is to evaluate incomplete modalities; pediatric POTS does not require routine chest imaging merely because this benchmark uses it. A different paper, MEDFuse (Phan et al., 2024), combines laboratory and note representations and reports disease classification on MIMIC-III and an institutional dataset.[16] The similar names refer to distinct methods.

Foresight (2024) extracts biomedical concepts from notes and models patient timelines with a generative transformer across two UK hospital datasets and MIMIC-III. Its paper defines its precision@10 result as whether at least one of ten candidates is correct.[17] This is not equivalent to ordinary per-candidate precision, a calibrated probability, or evidence that simulated interventions have causal validity.

Recent work narrows the remaining gap

TimeX (June 2026) extracts phenotype onset from narratives using a modular language-model pipeline. Its timestamp evaluation used 102 manually annotated notes and reported average accuracy of 81.24%; four rare-disease cohorts illustrated differences between narrated onset and documentation time.[18] Local extraction validation remains necessary, particularly when timing is vague or absent.

Kim and colleagues (September 2026) integrated note-derived events, structured EHR data, ontology mappings, and temporal relations into patient knowledge graphs. Physician adjudication evaluated extraction; a GLP-1 receptor agonist cohort illustrated longitudinal treatment-response analyses.[19] This is especially close architectural prior work. It demonstrates that integrated longitudinal patient graphs are feasible, while leaving pediatric POTS performance, GNN benefit, and causal treatment effects unestablished.

5. Why pediatric POTS changes the design

Preserve the distinction between definition and observation

POTS involves sustained excessive upright tachycardia with chronic orthostatic symptoms and exclusion of alternative explanations. Orthostatic means related to upright posture. For the proposed adolescent cohort, the operational primary definition follows Boris and Moak: symptoms for at least three months, sustained heart-rate increase of at least 40 beats/min within ten minutes upright, no sustained blood-pressure fall meeting the 20/10 mmHg systolic/diastolic orthostatic-hypotension threshold, and no other condition adequately explaining the tachycardia. Their review emphasizes that diagnosis also requires clinical context.[20]

Definitions are not uniform. The 2024 Chinese pediatric guideline describes a usual duration exceeding one month and permits age-specific maximum upright heart-rate thresholds alongside a 40-beat/min increase.[21] These differences can alter cohort membership. The study should retain component measurements and apply alternate definitions in sensitivity analyses. Restricting the primary cohort to ages 12–17 avoids treating younger-child criteria as settled. Anxiety or another comorbidity should not automatically erase a POTS phenotype; adjudication should assess whether an alternative explanation actually accounts for the tachycardia.

A single pulse measurement cannot retain posture, duration, blood-pressure response, symptoms during the test, or testing conditions. Store serial measurements, protocol, medication exposure, time of day, and the clinical interpretation separately. Likewise, keep suspected, confirmed, historical, and excluded diagnoses distinct. An unmentioned symptom is unknown, not absent.

Long-term outcomes already have clinical precedent

Tao and colleagues followed 115 of 121 pediatric patients after conventional management. The 2019 study used Kaplan–Meier and Cox analyses, with mean follow-up of 18.7 months and a range of 14–74 months. It reported cumulative symptom-free estimates of 48.4% at one year and 85.6% at six years.[22] The late estimate is not the proportion of all 115 participants directly observed symptom-free at six years. Follow-up distribution, endpoint definition, and treatment-related censoring matter.

The 2024 LT-POTS survey assessed 227 interpretable responses from patients diagnosed in a pediatric program; mean respondent age was 21.8 years. It included symptoms, quality of life, education, employment, and treatment history.[23] This establishes the relevance of functional outcomes, while response selection and recalled experience limit inference. Neither study establishes the causal effect of a therapy or a validated trajectory taxonomy. Different instruments and observation schedules also prevent simple pooling of “recovery'' estimates.

Diagnostic machine learning is related, but not equivalent

Gilmore et al. classified orthostatic-intolerance groups using tilt-test heart rate and blood pressure.[24] Akera and Novak's 2026 retrospective study reported AUROCs of 0.98/0.99 for models incorporating heart-rate data, compared with 0.68/0.63 for the Survey of Autonomic Symptoms alone.[25] These are the paper's model-specific results, not expected performance in community adolescents. Although diagnostic criteria were not supplied as explicit model rules, using the physiological measurements that help define the label creates a strong route to reproducing the diagnostic assessment. That is useful for a diagnostic-assistance task, but does not show that symptoms alone identify POTS early or forecast later disability.

6. A transparent representation of patient state

Separate clinical evidence from general knowledge

The proposed system should keep a versioned ontology layer separate from patient observations and from relations learned from training data. Diagnoses can use a clinical terminology such as SNOMED CT; symptoms can use HPO where an appropriate mapping exists; medication and measurement identifiers can use RxNorm and LOINC. Raw values, units, posture, and text evidence must remain accessible. A mapping is not permission to replace the source statement with a more specific diagnosis.

Figure 2a summarizes the representation. An observation should contain at least what was observed, when it occurred, when it became available, who reported it, and whether it was present, absent, uncertain, or resolved. Family history and patient history must be separated. Repeated copied text should not create artificial evidence that a symptom persists.

Two times are necessary

A note written in June may say that dizziness began in March. March is the reported event time; June is the information-availability time. A retrospective natural-history analysis may use the March estimate while recording its uncertainty. A prediction made in April cannot use that June note. TimeX provides relevant extraction precedent, but reported onset remains an estimate.[18]

Let Hᵢ(t) include only observations for patient i available by time t. A proposed patient representation is

zᵢ(t) = fθ(Hᵢ(t), Gknowledge, ageᵢ(t), gapsᵢ(t), masksᵢ(t))

Here fθ is a learned model, Gknowledge is the versioned knowledge graph, gaps are elapsed times, and masks indicate which measurements or modalities are missing. This notation describes an implementation choice, not an established POTS equation.

7. Proposed pediatric POTS research protocol

Question, cohort, and outcomes

Primary question. Among adolescents with adjudicated POTS, does an ontology-informed temporal graph representation improve prediction of month-6 physical function, using data available through month 3, beyond baseline function and equally informed nongraph models? This is a prognosis question. The separate descriptive aim is to identify reproducible patterns of symptom and functional change.

Recruit consecutive patients aged 12–17 across pediatric systems, recording referral source and exclusions. For diagnostic comparisons, use symptomatic non-POTS patients; retain vasovagal syncope, orthostatic hypotension, alternative explanations, and unresolved evaluations as distinct groups. Record comorbidities without assuming they explain all symptoms.

Two clinicians, blinded to predictions, should independently adjudicate a stratified sample; a third resolves disagreement. Report agreement, certainty, and unavailable diagnostic components. Validate automated labels against this reference, including uncoded cases. Account for enriched review sampling when estimating population predictive values.

Schedule assessments at baseline, 3, 6, and 12 months. Use the age-appropriate child self-report PedsQL 4.0 Physical Functioning scale, subject to permissions and patient review.[26] Its general pediatric validation does not establish POTS-specific responsiveness. The primary endpoint is the month-6 continuous score, adjusted for month-3 function; lock scoring before enrollment. Use a prespecified assessment window, such as ±30 days, and report actual timing. Secondary outcomes can include standardized symptom burden, school participation, orthostatic physiology, and acute-care use. Do not combine these into an unvalidated “recovery'' score. A deterioration threshold should be secondary unless a defensible clinically important difference is established for the selected instrument and population.

Data and extraction quality

Capture symptom presence, frequency, severity, triggers, and resolution where documented. Include fatigue, cognitive complaints, pain, gastrointestinal symptoms, orthostatic symptoms, sleep, and function without presuming fixed subtype boundaries. Record age, available pubertal information, medication changes, intercurrent infection, testing conditions, and care intensity. Separate patient reports from clinician observations. Wearable data are an optional substudy and require posture/activity context, device documentation, and consent; a pulse stream alone is not an orthostatic test.

Annotate a local sample enriched for negation, uncertain onset, copied notes, and discordant diagnoses. Evaluate concept mapping, assertion status, experiencer, and temporal extraction separately. Report precision and recall with patient-clustered uncertainty, exact versus hierarchical concept matches, and errors by site and symptom category. Validate onset intervals against reviewers' intervals instead of forcing vague dates into exact days. Preserve unmapped concepts for review rather than inventing ontology identifiers.

Sample size and feasibility

Estimate cohort size, outcome variance, attrition, and within-patient correlation before selecting model complexity. Simulate precision for between-model differences in prediction error, accounting for site structure. Repeated visits do not substitute for independent patients. For small cohorts, prioritize frozen embeddings or penalized models, extraction validation, and prospective outcome collection.

8. Baselines, controls, and evaluation

All comparisons should use the same eligible patients, outcome definitions, information cutoff, and tuning budget. Fit normalization, imputation, feature selection, co-occurrence graphs, and frequency weights using training patients only. Keep all visits from one patient in one partition. An ontology published outside the cohort may be shared, but knowledge versions and any test-patient contribution to pretraining must be disclosed.

Primary performance and transportability

For the continuous function endpoint, report mean absolute error, root mean squared error, calibration of predicted versus observed scores, and interval coverage if prediction intervals are produced. Estimate paired between-model differences using bootstrap resampling by patient, with site-aware analyses. Choose the model and all thresholds using development data before examining the locked test set.

For secondary binary outcomes, report prevalence, area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC), AUROC, sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, Brier score, and calibration. AUPRC depends on prevalence; AUROC alone can obscure clinically poor positive predictive value. Assess decision-curve net benefit only for a clearly specified action and plausible thresholds. Use a chronological test set and a genuinely held-out site. Report subgroup uncertainty by age, sex, race/ethnicity as recorded, referral route, and data completeness; small subgroups warrant uncertainty, not confident equivalence claims.

Validate trajectories separately from prediction

Fit descriptive state models using only the development cohort. Compare embedding-based clustering with mixed-effects trajectories or latent-class longitudinal models using the same observed domains. Test stability across bootstrap samples, random seeds, time-bin choices, and sites. Allow overlapping symptom dimensions and changing membership; a forced single cluster can hide concurrent problems.

Outcome prediction and subtype discovery should use separate representations or explicit safeguards. If an embedding is trained to predict function, finding function differences between its clusters is partly built into the method. Evaluate clusters against independently collected information excluded from fitting, and assess whether they persist after controlling for baseline severity and care intensity. Do not name clusters “neuropathic,'' “autoimmune,'' or “hyperadrenergic'' unless independent measurements support those labels. An attention map or visually separated embedding plot is insufficient.

Missing data, attrition, and treatment

Use scheduled patient-reported assessments to reduce dependence on visit frequency. Describe loss to follow-up by baseline characteristics, compare observed outcomes with weighted analyses where assumptions are plausible, and conduct sensitivity analyses for worse or better outcomes among missing participants. Imputation must not cross the prediction cutoff. Do not fill a missing symptom with “absent,'' and do not carry a finding forward indefinitely without a specified persistence rule.

Treat medication exposure as time-varying context. A prescription is not proof of adherence or benefit. Post-landmark treatment is part of the future care pathway; including it as a baseline predictor would leak future information. Associations between treatment and later outcomes are susceptible to confounding by indication: people who are more unwell may receive more treatment. A treatment-effect aim would require a separate target-trial-style protocol with eligibility, treatment strategies, time zero, confounders, follow-up, and identification assumptions specified in advance. Predictive embeddings alone do not resolve that problem.

9. What is genuinely missing?

The searches found no directly matching, externally validated pediatric POTS system combining ontology-aware temporal patient representations, multiple EHR modalities, and prediction of prospectively measured function. This is a bounded finding from a targeted review, not proof that no such paper exists. Several components already have strong precedents, as summarized below.

10. Discussion and practical priorities

The evidence supports assembling a modular system in which data quality can be evaluated independently from prediction. Ontology mapping can make concepts comparable, but it can also hide uncertainty if a broad phrase is mapped to an overly specific term. Graph structure can preserve relationships, but it can also encode a referral pattern. A temporal model can use elapsed time, but its apparent trajectory may still be driven by irregular observation. These risks are measurable and should be tested directly.

For pediatric POTS, the most valuable initial contribution may be a carefully adjudicated, time-resolved dataset and a reproducible phenotype specification. A smaller model that predicts function reliably and shows its supporting observations may be more useful than a larger model that mostly reconstructs specialist documentation. Conversely, graph methods would earn their added complexity if improvements persist against strong matched baselines, temporal and external validation, and realistic missing-data conditions.

A feasible sequence is to establish reliable case definitions and outcomes, validate extraction and mappings, benchmark static and temporal models, then introduce graph structure. Prospective silent validation should precede any clinical display: predictions are generated without influencing care and compared with subsequent outcomes. If useful, the next evaluation should test a specific workflow, such as prioritizing follow-up, rather than a general claim of “clinical utility.'' Study oversight should include adolescent and caregiver input, appropriate consent or authorization, access controls, and reproducible versioning of mappings and models.

Conclusion. Ontology embeddings, longitudinal phenotyping, graph-based prediction, and multimodal patient representations are established research areas. Their combination offers a plausible framework for pediatric POTS, but the clinical value is still an empirical question. The strongest study would demonstrate reliable timing and phenotype measurement, reproducible trajectories, and better prediction of subsequent function across health systems, with clear evidence that gains are not explained by diagnostic leakage or healthcare utilization.
